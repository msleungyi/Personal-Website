Many parents have shared with me the same doubt: their kids claim that they’re doing homework, and the next moment they’re gaming instead. Are electronic gadgets learning tools, or triggers for family conflicts?

I am happy to have Ms. Lam, a Chinese-language teacher with more than ten years of experience in Hong Kong primary schools, for an in-depth conversation on how to help children build healthy habits around digital devices at home. This blog post brings together some of the main points that we've discussed.

## How parents feel about digital homework

Based on Ms Lam’s experience teaching in Hong Kong primary schools, parents of older primary students tend to be more resistant to digital homework than parents of younger children. One common concern is that children will use “doing homework” as an excuse to play games or browse the internet.

Among parents of younger students, attitudes towards e-homework are more mixed. Some appreciate gamified digital homework for knowledge consolidation because children can often complete it without much parental help. But when an assignment requires a parent to help with recording audio or video, it can quickly become a burden.

Ms Lam made an important point: teachers are not necessarily expecting these assignments to be perfect. The purpose of an audio or video task may simply be to practise oral communication, rather than to produce a polished final product. If parents can let go of the expectation that every assignment needs to be flawless, the whole process can become much less stressful.

## Children need to be taught how to use digital learning tools

In this podcast episode, I also shared something I've noticed in Germany: many primary school children grow up using tablets but are surprisingly unfamiliar with typing on a keyboard because they're so used to touchscreens.

A similar phenomenon has been observed in Hong Kong, according to Ms Lam. Children may struggle with keyboards, logging into accounts, or even recognising the symbols used in passwords.

It is a useful reminder that being surrounded by technology does not automatically make a child digitally competent. Children still need to be taught how to use these tools, both in terms of practical skills and in terms of how to stay focused while using them.

## The real issue is self-control

When children use digital homework as an excuse to play games, an understandable response from parents is to ban the tablet altogether. Ms Lam, however, believes that an outright ban can easily backfire. It avoids the temptation rather than teaching children how to manage it.

One practical strategy she suggested is to create clear associations between particular places, times and activities. Homework might always be done at a desk or in a quiet corner, while entertainment takes place somewhere more relaxed, such as the sofa. A timer can also help children understand that different times and spaces are associated with different activities.

Building habits is a marathon, not a sprint. Rather than expecting children to follow every rule perfectly from the beginning, parents can start with a realistic standard, allow some distraction and mistakes, and gradually help them improve.

## Useful tips on effective reward systems

Ms Lam also shared an interesting principle for reward systems: some of the most meaningful rewards are things that money cannot buy.

For example, a child might earn the chance to choose what the family has for dinner, or enjoy some dedicated one-to-one time with a parent. Praise can also focus on the child’s effort and attitude rather than their marks, so that achievement does not become too closely tied to self-worth.

One thing she would avoid is using “allowing ten more minutes on the iPad” as a reward. Doing so can unintentionally make screen time seem even more desirable.

## Takeaways for teachers and parents

For teachers, Ms Lam suggested taking a careful look at digital homework before assigning it: check the accuracy of AI-generated materials, and ask whether a particular task genuinely needs to be digital in the first place.

For parents, some practical steps are equally simple. Try not to schedule digital homework during the hour before bedtime. And when family circumstances genuinely make it difficult to complete an assignment on time, communicate with the teacher rather than struggling in silence. In many cases, there may be room for flexibility.

Digital homework is increasingly popular in primary education, and that does not necessarily have to be a bad thing. With clear routines around time, place and rewards, learning to use digital tools well can also become an opportunity for children to practise self-regulation and gradually take more responsibility for their own learning.
